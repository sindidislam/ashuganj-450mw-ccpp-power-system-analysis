0First ideas: electricity, the plant, and why protection exists

0.1 Voltage, current, power — in one paragraph

Voltage is electrical pressure, current is the flow of charge, and power is pressure × flow: P = V × I. Bangladesh’s grid moves power at 230,000 V (230 kV) because high voltage means low current for the same power — and low current means thin wires and small losses. Your phone charger does the reverse: it steps voltage back down.

Plumbing analogy (used through this manual): voltage = water pressure, current = litres/second, a transformer = a pressure-exchange station, a circuit breaker = a valve that can slam shut under full pressure, a disconnector = a cap you unscrew only when the pipe is already empty.

0.2 AC and three phases

Grid power is AC: the pressure flips direction 50 times per second (50 Hz). It is three-phase: three pressures taking turns, 120° apart, sharing one job — like three people pushing a merry-go-round in rotation. Three wires carry smoother, stronger power than one. A short circuit (fault) is when phases touch each other (or earth) where they shouldn’t: pressure collapses there, and enormous current rushes in — tens of thousands of amperes. Our F1 fault reaches 126,210 A.

0.3 The plant in one sentence

One gas turbine + one steam turbine on one shaft → one 458 MVA generator at 22 kV → giant step-up transformer (GSUT) → 230 kV switchyard → national grid, with a side branch (UAT/GAT) stepping back down to 6.6 kV to run the plant’s own pumps and fans (the auxiliary load). “Combined cycle” just means the steam turbine re-uses the gas turbine’s exhaust heat.

0.4 Why protection exists

A fault melts metal in seconds. Protection is the plant’s reflex system: current transformers (CTs) shrink kilo-amperes to ~1 A so relays can measure safely; relays decide “fault or not” in milliseconds; circuit breakers interrupt the current; disconnectors only isolate dead equipment (never interrupt). Fast, selective primary protection (differentials: 87G/87B/7SD) clears first; slower backup overcurrent (51/51N) catches whatever the primary misses. This whole project is the engineering behind those reflexes.

Check yourself 0-A: A 230 MW load at 230 kV draws roughly what current? (Assume 3-phase, power factor 1.)
I = P/(√3·V) = 230e6/(1.732·230e3) ≈ 577 A. Our generator’s 12,019 A at 22 kV is the same idea at lower voltage — that’s why generator currents are huge.
Check yourself 0-B: Why can’t a disconnector replace a circuit breaker?
A disconnector has no arc-quenching: opening it under load draws a sustained arc. Breakers (Q0, 52G) are built to kill the arc; disconnectors (Q1/Q2/Q9) operate only dead. That’s why duty is never assigned to them.

✎Toolbox: the per-unit system (with worked example)

Power engineers divide every quantity by a chosen base so a 22 kV generator and a 230 kV grid can share one set of equations. This project uses Sbase = 100 MVA, Vbase = 230 kV.

Base quantities (one base, whole plant). Pick \(S_{base}=100\,\mathrm{MVA}\). At each voltage level \(V_{base}\): \[ Z_{base}=\frac{V_{base}^2}{S_{base}},\qquad I_{base}=\frac{S_{base}}{\sqrt{3}\,V_{base}} \] So at 230 kV: \(Z_{base}=230^2/100=\mathbf{529\,\Omega}\), \(I_{base}=100/(\sqrt{3}\cdot230)=\mathbf{0.251\,kA}\). At 22 kV: \(Z_{base}=22^2/100=\mathbf{4.84\,\Omega}\), \(I_{base}=\mathbf{2.62\,kA}\). same physics, two rulers
Worked example — rated current and per-unit power. \[ I_{rated}=\frac{458\times10^6}{\sqrt{3}\times22\times10^3}=\mathbf{12{,}019\,A},\qquad S_{pu}=\frac{458}{100}=\mathbf{4.58\,pu},\qquad X_{d,sat}''=22.48\%=\mathbf{0.2248\,pu} \] A transformer at “16%” is simply \(0.16\,\mathrm{pu}\) — volts and amps reappear only when you multiply back by base. try it: GSUT 515 MVA → 5.15 pu
Moving impedance across bases (used for every reactance in Phase 3). \[ Z_{pu,new}=Z_{pu,old}\times\frac{S_{new}}{S_{old}}\times\left(\frac{V_{old}}{V_{new}}\right)^2 \] Forget the voltage-squared term and a 22 kV device lands on the 230 kV base 109× too small — the classic beginner bug this project’s tests guard against. base-conversion tests
Analogy: per-unit is like quoting heights in “door-heights” instead of centimetres — a child and a basketball player can be compared with the same number line.
Check yourself PU: A 0.7 km line has 0.1426 Ω reactance. What is it in pu on the 529 Ω base?
Xpu = 0.1426/529 ≈ 0.00027 pu. Small numbers are normal in pu — that’s the point: the math stays well-behaved.

P1–2Phases 1–2 — Build the model, run the load flow

What is a load flow? (the plumbing calculation)

Given the generator’s push (P megawatts, Q megavars) and every load’s sip, compute the pressure (voltage) at every bus and the flow on every branch. It must balance: power in = power out + losses, checked at every bus (KCL, residuals < 0.05 MVA here). It must come first because every fault study starts from the pre-fault voltages — you can’t short-circuit a network you haven’t solved.

Three-phase power (the only load-flow formula you need first). \[ P=\sqrt{3}\,V_{LL}\,I\cos\phi,\qquad Q=\sqrt{3}\,V_{LL}\,I\sin\phi,\qquad S=\sqrt{P^2+Q^2} \] Check: \(S=\sqrt{360^2+27.83^2}\approx\mathbf{361.1\,MVA}\ll458\,\mathrm{MVA}\) — the 360 MW dispatch sips only ~79% of the machine. Load flow then enforces, at every bus: \(\sum P_{in}=\sum P_{out}+P_{losses}\) (same for \(Q\)), i.e. KCL in power form. conservation gate 6/6
P vs Q in one line: P (megawatts) does the real work — heat, motion. Q (megavars) builds the magnetic fields motors and transformers need to function. Generators must supply both, inside their capability curve.

What this project did

  • Modelled generator (458 MVA, capability curve with 6 points), GSUT 515 MVA, UAT/GAT 25 MVA, 0.7 km line, grid equivalent, 14 MW aux load.
  • Primary dispatch 360 MW (an operating point — not the 458 MVA nameplate, not the 389.3 MW Siemens rated power). A capacity guard rejects dispatches above 360 MW.
  • Solved two prefault states: LF360_GAT_OUT (B22 22.0 kV ∠−22.78°, Q 27.83 MVAr) and LF360_GAT_IN (Q 23.67 MVAr) — kept separate because their internal voltages differ.
  • Closed with 1331/0 tests and a frozen network both later phases inherit untouched.
Check yourself P2: The generator makes 360 MW but its nameplate says 458 MVA. Contradiction?
No. MVA is capacity (P and Q combined: S = √(P²+Q²)); 360 MW is today’s dispatch. A truck rated for 10 tons carrying 7 tons is not a contradiction.

P4Phase 4 — Fault analysis (the short-circuit laboratory)

The four fault types (learn this diagram)

Symmetrical components in one paragraph

Unbalanced faults (LG/LL/LLG) are hard in phase coordinates, so engineers split them into three balanced worlds — positive, negative, zero sequence — solve each (they’re just ordinary circuits), then add them back (Fortescue). The zero-sequence world is where grounding lives: our generator neutral resistor (NER ≈ 1750 Ω, i.e. 3ZN ≈ 5252 Ω) makes the zero world so “stiff” that only 7.27 A flows for an F1 ground fault. Count that impedance exactly once — the classic student trap is counting it twice.

Hand-check the 7.27 A: I0 ≈ Vph / 3ZN = 12701.7 / 5252.3 ≈ 2.418 mA per sequence → faulted phase 3I0 ≈ 7.25 A (code: 7.272 A ✓) you can do this on a calculator

How the three sequence worlds rejoin (the heart of Phase 4)

Each fault type stitches the sequence networks together differently. \(E''\) is the pre-fault internal voltage, \(Z_1, Z_2, Z_0\) the sequence impedances seen from the fault:

LLL (only the positive world): \(\displaystyle I_{1}=\frac{E''}{Z_1}\), \(I_2=I_0=0\)  ·  LG (all three in series): \(\displaystyle I_0=I_1=I_2=\frac{E''}{Z_0+Z_1+Z_2}\), faulted phase \(I_a=3I_0\)  ·  LL: \(\displaystyle I_1=-I_2=\frac{E''}{Z_1+Z_2}\), \(I_0=0\)  ·  LLG: \(Z_1\) in series with \(Z_2\parallel Z_0\) (parallel split, then series). Fortescue both directions, tested to 3e-14
Peak and duration extras (design-defined, informational). First-peak estimate \(i_p=\kappa\sqrt{2}\,I_k''\) with \(\kappa(r)=1.02+0.98e^{-3r}\), \(r=R_1/X_1\) (borrowed-shape curve, not an IEC claim). Breaker-window current \(I_b\) uses \(t_{break}=0.06\,\mathrm{s}\) study reference. Neither ever enters duty or margin arithmetic. phase4_kappa / phase4_stages
Branch vs total (the #1 student trap). A relay sees its branch current, not the bus total. On F1 LG the GEN branch carries 8,414 A while the net earth fault is 7.27 A — the branches vectorially cancel (KCL ~1e-12). Leg tables store |Ia| only, so LL/LLG (B–C faults) needed phasor recovery (|Ib| = 51.79 kA on F1 LL!). Never use a bus total as a relay current.
Check yourself P4: F1 LG total is 7.27 A but GEN_Q branch is 8,414 A. Broken math?
No — KCL closes on signed phasors, and magnitudes don’t sum. The branches carry large circulating currents that cancel to 7.27 mA-sequence at the fault node (residuals ~1e-12 prove it). The CT on the generator breaker really sees ~8.4 kA.

Differential protection in one equation

\[ I_{op}=|I_{in}+I_{out}|,\qquad I_{res}=\frac{|I_{in}|+|I_{out}|}{2},\qquad \text{trip if } I_{op}>\max(I_{pickup},\, \mathrm{slope}\times I_{res}) \] This is a simplified teaching characteristic with signed currents into the zone, after ratio and vector-group compensation. A complete relay model also needs CT errors, restraint, saturation and through-fault stability. Phase 5 does not implement that manufacturer algorithm: its 87G/87T/87B/7SD results are CONDITIONAL-DETECTABILITY. The 45-ms generator/transformer and 35-ms bus/line detection times are explicit study proxies, not measured clearing times.

Protection proxies and functional trips

F1/F2 single-line-to-ground (LG) faults use the approximately 7.27-A neutral residual for the proxy checks. The 87G and 87T starts are not reached; both are NO-TRIP for these rows. Dedicated 51N and the assumed 64G function provide the study's earth-protection representation. A conditional detectability result for another fault does not prove actual manufacturer relay operation.

The functional trip matrix specifies generator lockout/52G and excitation actions, transformer lockout/52G/Q0 actions, affected-bus breaker sets, and local/remote line-end equivalents. Exact commissioned wiring remains unverified. Q0 is the qualified GSUT transformer-bay identity; a line-end breaker is not silently mapped onto it.

Distance backup reaches are 0.0448+j0.196, 0.0672+j0.294 and 0.112+j0.490 Ω, with 0/0.30/0.80-s study timers. Breaker-failure timers are 0.15 s at 230 kV and 0.12 s at 22 kV. The 64G study defaults are 1 V / 10 mA, 20-Ω trip / 100-Ω alarm, 1-s trip / 10-s alarm and 0° correction; manufacturer-default origin awaits the local manual and primary-injection verification.

Check yourself P5: why does NO-PAIR not necessarily mean no protection?
For F1 LG with GAT out, GEN-51N sees 7.272 A / 20 = 0.3636 A secondary, above its 0.20-A pickup. Its calculated SI time is 1.746 s. The coordination row is NO-PAIR because no upstream residual relay is modeled across the YNd1 delta. This is distinct from NO-TRIP, and neither verdict proves installed plant performance.
