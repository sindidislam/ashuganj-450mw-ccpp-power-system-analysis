⚡ Teacher Defense: Why Voltage is Zero in Bolted LLL/LLLG Faults CRITICAL QUESTION ANSWERED

"Sir's Question: 'Why is voltage zero in the LLL / LLLG fault? Voltage should not be zero! Show the physics and calculations.'"
Screen Action Show the math derivation slide or the Sequence Network diagram from Chapter 5 of the Report Writing Manual.
🗣️ Spoken Words (Natural & Clear Explanation)

"Now, I want to specifically address an important technical point our course teacher pointed out: 'Why does voltage become zero during an LLL or LLLG fault? Shouldn't there be some voltage left?'

The answer is very clear and comes down to two simple things:

1. In fault analysis, we assume a 'Bolted' Fault ($Z_f = 0$):
When testing switchgear ratings under IEC 60909, we must test the absolute worst-case scenario. That means we assume the three phases touch each other and ground directly with zero contact resistance—what power engineers call a 'bolted fault' ($Z_f = 0$).
By basic Ohm's law: $$\text{Voltage} = \text{Current} \times \text{Resistance}$$ If the resistance between the conductors is zero, the voltage across them *must mathematically drop to zero*!

In sequence networks, the positive-sequence bus voltage is: $$V_k^{(1)} = V_{\text{prefault}} - Z_{kk} \times I_{\text{fault}}$$ Because the fault current is $I_{\text{fault}} = \frac{V_{\text{prefault}}}{Z_{kk}}$, substituting it gives: $$V_k^{(1)} = V_{\text{prefault}} - Z_{kk} \left(\frac{V_{\text{prefault}}}{Z_{kk}}\right) = V_{\text{prefault}} - V_{\text{prefault}} = \mathbf{0.000\text{ Volts}}$$ So right at the bolted fault bus, the voltage collapses completely to zero.

2. So why do people sometimes expect voltage NOT to be zero?
In real life, voltage is only non-zero for two reasons:
• Arc Resistance: In a real flashover, the electric arc isn't solid metal; it has about 10 to 50 milli-ohms of arc resistance. Across 126 kA, that arc drops around 2,500 Volts (roughly 10% to 15% voltage).
• Distance from the fault: Voltage is zero only right at the fault bus. If you measure at an adjacent substation bus or behind a transformer, the voltage recovers to 30%, 50%, or 70% based on the impedance divider.
Because our simulation probe was placed directly on the bolted fault node, measuring exactly 0.000 per-unit confirms our model strictly satisfies standard IEC 60909 boundary conditions."

Bonus Answer: Can LLG Fault Current Ever Be Higher Than 3-Phase (LLL)?
Yes, it can! In power systems, when the Zero-Sequence Impedance is smaller than Positive-Sequence Impedance ($Z_0 < Z_1$), the parallel combination $Z_2 \parallel Z_0$ is smaller than $Z_1$. That actually makes the LLG phase current higher than the 3-phase fault current! This happens in solidly grounded switchyards near big Delta-Wye transformers.
However, in our 22 kV generator island, because of our 1,750 $\Omega$ grounding resistor, $Z_0$ is enormous, so 3-phase fault ($126.2\text{ kA}$) is way larger than any ground fault.
